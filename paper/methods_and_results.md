## Abstract

**Purpose** JIGSAWS sanctions two cross-validation protocols for surgical skill
assessment: one holds out a repetition while every surgeon remains in training, the other
holds out a surgeon entirely. We ask what a model evaluated under the first measures, and
whether reported confidence would support deferring uncertain trials to a human rater.

**Methods** Two prediction paths were evaluated on identical information across 103 trials,
8 surgeons and 3 tasks: 16 kinematic features with ridge and multinomial logistic
regression, and a zero-shot commercial decision model given the same features rendered as
text. Both protocols were run. Errors are reported against a feature-free reference
predicting each held-out trial with its training-fold mean rating; intervals resample whole
surgeons. Analysis rules were fixed in advance; post-hoc additions are labelled.

**Results** Changing only the protocol removed at least a third of skill-classification
accuracy (paired gap 0.49-0.67, intervals excluding zero). A post-hoc diagnostic found the same
features identified which of eight surgeons performed a held-out trial with accuracy
0.83-0.93 against chance near 0.13, separating identity confounding from underfitting.
On unseen surgeons one task of three beat the reference, and there a single-feature
regression on trial duration matched both models. Both paths were overconfident (confidence
0.755 against accuracy 0.379; 0.696 against 0.301), scoring worse than a constant forecast,
and neither identified deferrable trials.

**Conclusion** Accuracy reported under the permissive protocol substantially reflects
surgeon recognition rather than skill measurement. With eight surgeons these are
single-dataset demonstrations rather than established generalities. We recommend reporting
held-out-surgeon results against an uninformative reference, with a subject-identifiability
diagnostic.

**Keywords** Surgical skill assessment; JIGSAWS; identity confounding; cross-validation;
calibration; selective prediction

## 1. Introduction

Automated assessment of surgical technical skill is motivated by the prospect of scalable,
consistent feedback during training. If such a system is to inform training or
credentialling decisions, it must generalise to surgeons who were not seen during its
development. Whether current evaluation practice on the field's standard benchmark
establishes that it does is the question we take up.

The concern is not specific to surgery. When a model is trained and tested on data from the
same individuals, it can succeed by recognising the individual rather than the property of
interest — a failure mode named identity confounding [Chaibub Neto et al. 2019] and
demonstrated systematically by Saeb et al. [2017], whose activity-recognition error stayed
near 2% when records from the same subjects appeared on both sides of the split, against
roughly 7% when whole subjects were held out and 27% when only two subjects were available.
Electroencephalography supplies the closest structural parallel to what we report below: an
Alzheimer's classifier reaching 99.8% accuracy under segment-based holdout fell to 53.0% on
unseen subjects, with a confidence interval spanning chance, and a seizure classifier fell
from 79.1% to 65.1% [Brookshire et al. 2024]. The same pattern appears in speech, where
depression detection degrades sharply on unseen speakers because the acoustic features are
entangled with speaker identity. These are instances of shortcut learning [Geirhos et al.
2020], and leakage of this general kind has been documented across 17 fields, collectively
affecting 294 papers [Kapoor and Narayanan 2023]. We return to the methodological debate
this literature provoked in §2.3.

The benchmark itself ships two cross-validation protocols: one holds out a repetition while
every surgeon remains in training, the other holds out a surgeon entirely. Both are
sanctioned and both appear in this literature, so what follows is not a criticism of an
informal practice. That performance falls under the second is not in dispute; it has been
reported repeatedly. What has not settled is why. Three studies
of JIGSAWS attribute the same gap to three different causes: limited generalisation,
answered by a call for surgeon-invariant features; the quality of the dataset's
representations and annotations; and an absence of subject-independent validation in
earlier work. A fourth, on open-surgery hand tracking rather than robot kinematics, comes
closest to the account we pursue, describing its own gap as quantifying "the
surgeon-specific component captured by video-level splits" — and stops there. Three
different attributions, none of them measured, and a fourth study that names a mechanism
without measuring it either. The test is inexpensive: train the same features to predict
which surgeon performed the trial, and read off how much identity is recoverable. To our
knowledge no surgical skill assessment study has run it. The gap has been treated as a
limitation to engineer away rather than as a quantity to measure.

Judging such a system also requires care about the target it is fitted to. The reference
standard is a human rating, and human ratings of technical skill are neither perfectly
reliable nor uniformly so: pooled inter-rater reliability for global rating scales in
simulation-based assessment has been reported at ICC 0.78 [Ilgen et al. 2015], while
untrained raters have been measured at 0.26, with the benefit of rater training lost during
periods of non-use [Gawad et al. 2019]. On this benchmark specifically, the self-declared
skill labels track the expert ratings poorly [Hendricks et al. 2024]. A model here is
therefore fitted to a noisy and training-dependent judgment rather than to a fixed standard.
Two consequences follow for how results should be read. Agreement statistics cannot be
weighed against a single fixed bar. And a reported accuracy or correlation is not
self-interpreting: it needs an explicit comparison with a predictor that uses no input at
all, a point we return to in §3.5 with a concrete demonstration of how misleading its
absence can be.

This paper asks what a skill model evaluated on JIGSAWS is actually measuring, and whether
the confidence such a model reports would support the human-in-the-loop use these systems
are proposed for. We evaluate two prediction paths on identical information — a
conventional baseline of 16 kinematic features with ridge and logistic regression, and a
commercial zero-shot decision model given the same features rendered as text — under both
protocols the benchmark sanctions. We make four contributions. First, we quantify the
protocol gap with paired surgeon-level intervals: changing only the evaluation protocol,
with features, model and data held constant, removes at least a third of the reported
skill-classification accuracy (§4.1). Second, in a post-hoc diagnostic, we show that the
same features identify which of eight surgeons performed a held-out trial with accuracy
between 0.83 and 0.93 against chance rates near 0.13, which distinguishes identity
confounding from the underfitting explanation that a performance gap alone leaves open
(§4.2). Third, we report what is to our knowledge the first calibration and
selective-prediction audit of a surgical skill assessment model built from motion data,
finding both paths overconfident on unseen surgeons and neither path's confidence able to
identify the trials it gets wrong (§4.5, §4.6). Fourth, we benchmark both against a
single-feature regression on trial duration, which matches them on the one task of three
where any cross-surgeon signal survives (§4.4). We do not claim to have discovered identity
confounding: the mechanism is well established elsewhere, and what we contribute is its
demonstration, quantification and consequences in this setting.

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 The benchmark and its two sanctioned protocols

JIGSAWS [Gao et al. 2014] is the standard public benchmark for robot-assisted surgical
skill assessment, and the accompanying benchmark suite [Ahmidi et al. 2017] defines the two
cross-validation schemes used throughout this literature. Leave-one-supertrial-out holds out
the *i*-th repetition across all surgeons; leave-one-user-out holds out every trial from one
surgeon. Both ship with the dataset and both are sanctioned, so the protocol we examine is
the benchmark's own rather than an informal practice.

The field is aware that individual style exists. A review of gesture recognition in robotic
surgery [van Amsterdam et al. 2021] notes that leave-one-user-out is the harder setting "due
to subject-specific style variability", and surveys methods that normalise such variability
away. That framing treats identity as nuisance variance to be suppressed in pursuit of a
better model. Our concern is the reverse: the same variability, under a protocol that places
a surgeon on both sides of the split, does not degrade a score but inflates it.

### 2.2 Label validity and protocol validity are separate critiques

The closest existing critique of skill assessment on this benchmark concerns the target.
Hendricks et al. [2024] show that the self-declared novice/intermediate/expert label
correlates poorly with the expert GRS rating, and "Looking Beyond the Scale" [2026] reports
the same mismatch. Our own data reproduce it — one surgeon labelled expert averages GRS 13.5
while one labelled novice averages 19.1 (§3.1) — and we report that as a replication of
their finding rather than as one of ours.

The distinction from our contribution is worth stating precisely, because the two are easily
conflated. **Hendricks et al. ask whether the target measures skill. We ask whether the
evaluation protocol permits a model to succeed without measuring skill at all, whatever the
target's quality.** These critiques are independent, and they compound. A perfectly valid
target would not repair what we describe: surgeon identifiability is a property of the
feature representation and the fold structure, not of what the label means, so a model could
shortcut a flawless label just as readily. Conversely their critique survives a flawless
protocol, since a well-designed split cannot rescue a target that measures the wrong thing.
A reader persuaded by Hendricks et al. should not conclude that fixing the label would fix
the benchmark.

### 2.3 The performance gap is widely reported, and framed three other ways

That accuracy falls under leave-one-user-out is not news in this literature. What differs is
the explanation offered.

I3D-SAP [IJCARS] reports near-perfect supertrial-out accuracy across the three tasks, a
significant drop under user-out evaluation, and near-chance transfer to an external dataset,
concluding that generalisation to unseen surgeons is limited and calling for
"surgeon-invariant feature learning"; the goal is named, the mechanism is not measured.
"Looking Beyond the Scale" [2026] evaluates GRS prediction under a strict
disjoint-participant protocol and reports a concordance correlation of -0.03 (+/-0.08) for a
supervised model trained and evaluated entirely on JIGSAWS, below chance, against 0.22 for
its best baselines; having ruled out model-side causes through an extensive architecture
and hyperparameter search, it attributes the failure to the annotations, which it reports
"lack a consistent ordering with respect to the visual features that encode actual skill
differences".
A recent explainable-machine-learning study using rich kinematic features [Journal of
Robotic Surgery, 2026] criticises prior JIGSAWS work for lacking subject-independent
validation — the same complaint, again without a direct test. Lefor et al. [2020] earlier
flagged the dataset's subject imbalance and an inverse correlation among subjects as a
threat to generalisation, without quantifying either.

Closest to our own framing is a markerless hand-tracking study of open-surgery suturing
[2026], which finds accuracy falling to 52.3% under leave-one-subject-out and states that
this gap "quantifies the surgeon-specific component captured by video-level splits". We
should be plain about how little separates that sentence from our thesis: they articulate
the same interpretation of the same kind of gap, and had they run a subject-identification
probe, the attribution would already exist in this literature. Our contribution relative to
that study is correspondingly narrower than relative to the others above — a different
modality, robot kinematics rather than open-surgery hand tracking, and a direct measurement
of the component they name but do not test. Taken together these studies establish that the
field suspects a subject-specific confound; none tests it directly.

The null in "Looking Beyond the Scale" overlaps our §4.3, and that overlap strengthens
rather than duplicates the contribution. Their study and ours reach the same conclusion
about cross-participant GRS prediction through almost entirely different routes: different
feature representations, different model families, a different agreement metric, and a
different reference against which the result is judged. A single null is always vulnerable
to the objection that it reflects an idiosyncrasy of one pipeline — an underpowered search,
an unlucky architecture, a poorly chosen metric. Two independent pipelines arriving at the
same place is much harder to explain that way, and their architecture search excludes
model-side causes that our single linear model could not have excluded alone. Independent
corroboration by a different method is better evidence than either result on its own. What
their study does not supply, and what we add, is the attribution: they establish that the
signal is absent, while we measure where the apparent signal under the permissive protocol
comes from.

One counter-argument must be met directly, because it applies to every study above and to
our §4.1. Responding to Saeb et al. [2017], Little et al. [2017] argued that a gap between
record-wise and subject-wise cross-validation can arise from *underfitting* on heterogeneous
data rather than from identity confounding, so that a gap alone establishes nothing about
mechanism. The objection is sound, and none of the surgical studies above can answer it,
because each reports only a gap. Our §4.2 is designed to answer it: the same 16 features,
the same model and the same folds identify which of eight surgeons performed a held-out
repetition with accuracy between 0.83 and 0.93, against chance rates near 0.13. A
performance gap is ambiguous between confounding and underfitting; recoverable identity at
roughly seven times chance is not.

To our knowledge, no surgical skill assessment study both attributes the protocol gap to
surgeon-identity leakage and demonstrates it by classifying the surgeon from the features
used for skill. We state this as the negative finding of a targeted search rather than as a
guarantee.

### 2.4 Reported performance, and the missing reference

In the studies we examined [Zia and Essa 2018; Wang et al. 2021; VBA-Net 2022], results
are reported as accuracies or as correlations between predicted and expert scores, without
the value the same metric would take for a predictor using no motion information at all. We
have not surveyed this literature systematically enough to call that a field-wide norm; it
is the pattern in the work we read. We depart from it deliberately, for the reason given in
§3.5 and §3.7: under leave-one-user-out, a predictor that returns its
training fold's mean GRS scores approximately -0.8 on pooled rank correlation purely by
construction. A positive correlation is therefore not self-interpreting, and a model can
post one while committing larger absolute errors than that uninformative predictor.

### 2.5 Calibration and selective prediction: a borrowed lineage

Calibration has begun to receive attention in surgery: SurgiQ [2026] evaluates how well
large language models are calibrated on surgical knowledge question-answering. That is a
different setting from ours. We located no calibration or selective-prediction study of a
surgical *skill assessment* model built from motion, kinematic or video data, and surgical
work on confidence-gated deferral is correspondingly thin. The
methodological lineage we draw on is therefore from diagnostic imaging and intensive care,
where deferral is established and demonstrably useful: CoDoC [Dvijotham et al. 2023] reduces
false positives by 25% at matched sensitivity while cutting clinician workload, and "Trust
or Escalate" [ICLR 2025] provides calibrated selective evaluation with provable agreement
guarantees. Overconfidence in model-as-judge settings is itself an active topic [Tian et al.
2025]. We take from this literature one caution that bears directly on our own null result:
deferral cannot manufacture reliability that the underlying model lacks ["Calibrated Triage,
Not Autonomy", 2026].

### 2.6 Zero-shot and text-based scoring

Foundation models have reached this benchmark, but not in the role we examine. The nearest
work, ZEAL, uses zero-shot video segmentation to produce features for a *trained* LSTM, so
the learning still happens downstream; that is a different mechanism from a decision model
scoring a rubric with no training examples at all. The model we evaluate, Jev, is marketed
on calibration — its training method is branded Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated
Decisions — though a trade critique notes the term is coined and that its "zero
hallucinations" claim concerns schema conformance rather than correctness [Eliot 2026].
Rather than rely on vendor claims, we note three independent evaluations published in
September 2026 [priorbench; scienthoon; webofmike] converging on a consistent pattern:
calibration holds on in-distribution data and degrades markedly out of distribution, and
one reports that the model's separate confidence field is less informative than its maximum
class probability. We give no figures from these evaluations, for the reason that
follows. **All three are non-peer-reviewed single-author repositories
or blog posts, and we cite them as preliminary external evidence rather than settled
results**; they also identify the model by version strings that may not correspond exactly
to the build we pinned, so we treat them as evidence about the model family rather than as
replications of our setup. The pattern they describe is the one our §4.5 reports on unseen
surgeons.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Dataset

See Table 1.

We use JIGSAWS, a public dataset of robot-assisted surgical training trials recorded on a
da Vinci system at 30 Hz. Eight surgeons performed three bench-top tasks — suturing,
knot-tying and needle-passing — with up to five repetitions each. Each trial carries an
expert rating on a modified OSATS instrument: six elements scored 1-5, whose sum is the
global rating score (GRS), range 6-30. Each surgeon also carries a self-declared skill
label (novice, intermediate, expert) reflecting hours of robotic experience.

The per-task metadata files, rather than the directory listing, are authoritative for
which trials are usable: the kinematics directories contain 40 files per task, but the
metadata files list 39 suturing, 36 knot-tying and 28 needle-passing trials, 103 in total.
The surplus recordings are excluded, and one of them is empty. Surgeon G contributes no
usable needle-passing trial, so that task has seven surgeons rather than eight. We
verified that GRS equals the sum of its six elements on all 103 rows, with no exceptions,
which is what identifies the columns; the ordering of the six elements among themselves
rests on the dataset's own readme and cannot be confirmed arithmetically, so we report no
per-element result.

Per-task GRS standard deviations are 5.40 (suturing), 5.11 (knot-tying) and 4.82
(needle-passing). These set the scale against which prediction errors below should be
read. We note that the self-declared skill label tracks the expert GRS rating poorly: one
surgeon labelled expert has a mean GRS of 13.5 while one labelled novice has 19.1
(`addendum_label_vs_grs`). The two targets are therefore reported separately throughout rather
than treated as interchangeable.

*Source: dataset facts are enforced by assertions in `src/loaders.py` and its tests rather
than logged as experiments.*

### 3.2 Features

We compute 16 descriptive motion features per trial from the patient-side manipulator
channels — the instruments inside the workspace — rather than the surgeon-side console
channels. Features are computed over the annotated task window, defined as the first
gesture start to the last gesture end from the trial's transcription; this window matches
the one embedded in the official split filenames on all 39 suturing trials.

Tool-tip positions are low-pass filtered once, before any derived quantity, with a
zero-phase Butterworth filter at 5 Hz (second order per pass, applied forward and
backward, so the gain at the cutoff is 0.5 and no time shift is introduced). The cutoff
was selected to sit below the 8-12 Hz physiological tremor band and above the
low-frequency content of voluntary movement; no JIGSAWS-specific value exists in the
literature, so the choice is a judgment within a defensible range and is accompanied by
sensitivity runs at no filtering and at 8 Hz.

The feature set comprises total task time; and per instrument, path length, mean and
standard deviation of speed, mean acceleration magnitude, the fraction of time below a
1 mm/s motion threshold, convex-hull working volume, and the number of gripper open-close
cycles; plus the correlation between the two instruments' speed profiles. Speed is
computed by finite differences of position rather than from the recorded velocity
channels, so it can be validated against hand-computed synthetic trajectories; on one
trial the two agree at Pearson r = 0.88 and 0.93 for the two instruments, with
finite-difference means within 5% of the recorded means (`addendum_speed_validation`).

All thresholds were fixed in `configs/features.yaml` before use and were never adjusted
against any outcome. Three features that appeared in our initial plan were removed before
modelling, each for a stated reason unrelated to predictive performance: maximum speed and
maximum acceleration, because both are corrupted by single-frame position discontinuities
(the largest is 26.9 mm within one 1/30 s frame against a typical 0.2 mm, and 47 of 206
arm-trials contain a step above 5 mm; `addendum_position_jumps`);
spectral arc length, because whole-trial values correlate -0.83 with movement count and
-0.81 with trial duration and therefore do not isolate smoothness
(`addendum_sparc_confound`); and
movement count, because threshold crossings remain unstable after filtering.

*Source: `configs/features.yaml`; feature-matrix construction is tested in
`tests/test_features.py`.*

### 3.3 Two prediction paths

**Baseline.** Ridge regression predicts GRS and multinomial logistic regression predicts
the three-way skill label. Features are standardised inside a pipeline so that scaling
parameters are estimated from training trials only. The ridge penalty is selected within
each training set by leave-one-subject-out search over {0.1, 1, 10, 100}, which prevents
the inner search from rewarding a model that recognises an individual surgeon's style. The
logistic regulariser is fixed at C = 1.0 rather than searched: with two experts and two
intermediates, a subject-level inner search can leave a training set containing no
instance of a class, making the search ill-defined. This asymmetry between the two models
follows from the data rather than from preference.

**Zero-shot decision model.** The second path uses Jev (TypeSafe, model version pinned to
`jev-1.13.0`), a commercial model that answers typed questions against a supplied state and
returns a score with a probability per level and a confidence value. It is never trained.
Because the model's documentation states that it does not count reliably and cannot judge
whether two numeric values are close, no number is transmitted: each of the 16 features is
rendered in code as one of five ordinal phrases describing its percentile within the same
task ("typical of comparable attempts", "higher than most comparable attempts", and so on).
The state contains no trial identifier, no surgeon letter, no repetition index, no label
and no digit; tests enforce each of these conditions. Seven scoring questions — one overall
and six OSATS elements, each with five ordered level descriptions — are sent in a single
request per trial. Each trial was scored three times, giving 309 requests. Every raw
response is cached to disk before parsing, and the model version reported in each response
is checked against the pinned version on every read.

The rubric wording was authored before any trial was scored and was not revised
afterwards. It was, however, written after we had seen the feature distributions and the
baseline results, and it refers to completion time and pauses; this is a disclosed route
by which the model's output could come to resemble a duration measure, and it bears on the
interpretation in §4.4.

*Source: `configs/baseline.yaml`, `configs/jev.yaml`; `kind=regression`,
`kind=classification`.*

### 3.4 Evaluation protocol

JIGSAWS defines two cross-validation schemes. Leave-one-supertrial-out (LOSO) holds out
the *i*-th repetition across all surgeons, so every surgeon appears in both training and
test data. Leave-one-user-out (LOUO) holds out all trials from one surgeon, so the test
surgeon is unseen. Pre-computed skill-detection splits ship only for suturing; for the
other two tasks we generate folds by the same documented rule. That generator reproduces
the official suturing folds exactly — identical training and test membership in all five
LOSO and all eight LOUO folds — which is verified by a test, so the generated folds
instantiate the standard definition rather than approximating it.

Every trial is predicted exactly once per scheme, by a model fitted without it. We report
both schemes throughout. The contrast between them is the paper's primary object of study.

*Source: `src/loaders.py`; fold equivalence is pinned by
`test_generated_splits_match_official_suturing`.*

### 3.5 Reference predictor and the headline error metric

Predictive claims are stated against an explicit reference rather than against zero. The
**feature-free reference** predicts every held-out trial with the mean GRS of its own
training fold; it uses no motion information whatsoever. A model has demonstrated
something only if its error falls below this reference's error.

Our headline quantity is therefore the **reduction in mean absolute error relative to that
reference**, in GRS points, positive meaning the model is better. This metric replaced the
one we pre-registered, for a reason given in §3.7.

**Interpretation rule, fixed before any interval was computed:** a method beats the
reference only if the lower bound of the 95% interval on its MAE reduction lies above zero.
With eight surgeons this is a weak test, and we treat "excludes zero" as suggestive rather
than as proof; no analysis selects models by it.

To place Jev's ordinal score on the GRS scale, a single ordinary-least-squares line is
fitted within each training fold and applied to the held-out trials, so the calibration
uses no held-out information. Because a fitted line would silently rescue a score that
ranked skill backwards, we record the sign of the fitted slope in every fold as a direction
guard.

*Source: `kind=headline_mae_vs_reference`, `kind=jev_headline_mae_vs_reference`,
`kind=diagnostic_feature_free_reference`.*

### 3.6 Uncertainty

Trials from one surgeon are not independent, so all intervals come from a bootstrap that
resamples **whole surgeons** with replacement (2000 resamples, seed 0), never individual
trials. Predictions are held fixed during resampling. An interval therefore expresses
which surgeons happened to be evaluated; it does **not** express how a result would move
if the model were refitted on different training data, and it does not capture that the
eight surgeons are themselves a small and possibly unrepresentative sample. Resamples in
which a statistic is undefined are counted and reported rather than silently dropped; for
every regression result reported here that count is zero. For the paper's central claim we
additionally bootstrap the LOSO-minus-LOUO difference **paired on the same resampled
surgeons**, which is more informative than comparing two independently computed intervals.

*Source: `src/metrics.py`; the whole-surgeon property is pinned by
`test_bootstrap_resamples_whole_surgeons_never_individual_trials`.*

### 3.7 Protocol scrutiny

Two findings about our own protocol belong in the record, and they are distinct.

**Leakage was tested for and was not found.** Scaling parameters and hyperparameters are
estimated inside training folds only, and held-out trials are never seen by any fitted
object. Two tests verify this behaviourally rather than by inspection: replacing the labels
of a test fold with nonsense leaves that fold's predictions bit-identical, and altering
some test-fold features leaves the predictions for the remaining test trials unchanged. To
confirm the tests can detect the failure they are designed for, a deliberately leaky
variant — standardising all rows before splitting — moved untouched test-fold predictions
by 16 GRS points where the correct pipeline moved them by exactly zero. We additionally
refitted the regression 50 times per task and scheme under shuffled labels; every mean rank
correlation was negative, which is the opposite of the direction leakage produces.

**Separately, our pre-registered headline statistic proved uninterpretable under LOUO and
was replaced.** We had pre-registered the pooled out-of-fold Spearman correlation. Under
LOUO each fold contains one surgeon and the prediction is one constant per fold; surgeons
differ in mean GRS, and a surgeon with a high mean receives a lower training-fold mean, so
prediction and truth move in opposite directions across folds by construction. Measured,
the feature-free reference scores -0.78 to -0.86 on this statistic. A LOUO correlation
therefore cannot be read against zero. We report the MAE reduction of §3.5 as the headline
and retain the correlations, always beside the reference value, never alone.

No result was altered by either finding. The first is a negative check that passed; the
second is a change of metric, and it is a post-hoc change (§4, preamble). We also note that
the shuffled-label criterion we wrote was two-sided and so flagged two of six cells in the
harmless negative direction; it was replaced with a one-sided criterion in a second
configuration, with the original flags left on the record.

*Source: `kind=permutation_sentinel`, `kind=diagnostic_intercept_only`; leakage behaviour
is pinned by tests in `tests/test_baseline.py`.*

## 4. Results

Every subsection below is labelled **[pre-declared]** where the analysis was specified
before the results were seen, or **[post-hoc]** where it was added afterwards to explain
something already observed. In summary: the protocol comparison (§4.1), the calibration
analysis (§4.5) and the selective-prediction analysis (§4.6) were pre-declared; the
surgeon-identifiability diagnostic (§4.2), the task-time comparison (§4.4) and the
headline error metric itself (§4.3) were not. The distinction is load-bearing for a paper
that examines protocol discipline, so it is carried in the text and not only in a table.

### 4.1 The protocol gap [pre-declared]

Skill classification accuracy under LOSO, where every surgeon appears in training, is 0.92
(suturing, 95% interval 0.85-0.98), 0.97 (knot-tying, 0.92-1.00) and 0.93
(needle-passing, 0.85-1.00). Under LOUO, where the test surgeon is unseen, the same
features and the same model give 0.44 (0.25-0.63), 0.31 (0.08-0.58) and 0.39 (0.16-0.69).

The drop is the paper's central observation, and it is best expressed as a paired
difference. Bootstrapping the LOSO-minus-LOUO gap on the same resampled surgeons gives
+0.49 (0.35-0.62) for suturing, +0.67 (0.41-0.89) for knot-tying and +0.54 (0.27-0.71) for
needle-passing. All three intervals exclude zero by a wide margin. Changing only the
evaluation protocol, with the features, the model and the data held constant, removes at
least a third of the reported accuracy, and plausibly more than half; the point estimates
are 0.49 to 0.67 but the intervals reach down to 0.27.

The LOUO point estimates fall at or below the rate obtainable by always predicting the
most frequent class in that task — 0.487, 0.444 and 0.393 respectively, so suturing and
knot-tying fall below and needle-passing lands exactly on it. **We state this as a point
estimate and not as a demonstration.** The LOUO intervals are wide enough to span the
majority-class rate in all three tasks, so with eight surgeons we cannot establish that
new-surgeon accuracy is below that floor; we can establish only that it is far below the
LOSO figure. Quadratic-weighted kappa is negative under LOUO for suturing (-0.19) and
needle-passing (-0.47), indicating agreement worse than chance on those two tasks, against
+0.77 and +0.80 under LOSO.

`kind=addendum_majority_class`, `kind=classification` (Table T2).*

### 4.2 The same features identify the surgeon [post-hoc]

This analysis was added after observing the gap in §4.1, in order to explain it. It was not
part of the pre-registered plan, and it should be read as a diagnostic rather than as a
confirmatory test.

Substituting surgeon identity for skill as the target, and leaving the features, the model,
the folds and the regularisation unchanged, the same 16 features identify which of eight
surgeons performed a held-out repetition with accuracy 0.87 (95% interval 0.77-0.97) for
suturing, 0.83 (0.67-0.97) for knot-tying and 0.93 (0.83-1.00) for needle-passing, against
chance rates of 0.125, 0.125 and 0.143. All three intervals lie far above chance.

Because the skill label is a property of the surgeon and is constant across that surgeon's
trials, a model that can identify the surgeon can retrieve the label without representing
skill. This is a sufficient mechanism for the LOSO figures in §4.1, and it is consistent
with their collapse under LOUO, where identity carries no usable information about an
unseen individual. It is not proof that the classifier relies on identity: we have
demonstrated that the information is present and recoverable, not that this particular
model uses it. Distinguishing the two would require an intervention on the features, which
we did not perform.

`kind=diagnostic_surgeon_identity`.*

### 4.3 GRS prediction against a feature-free reference [pre-declared design, post-hoc metric]

The design of this analysis was pre-registered; the metric reported was not, for the reason
given in §3.7. We flag that explicitly because the substitution is favourable to
readability and could otherwise pass unnoticed.

Under LOUO, ridge regression reduces mean absolute error relative to the feature-free
reference by +1.54 GRS points (95% interval 0.65-2.52) on knot-tying, from 5.10 to 3.56.
On suturing the reduction is +0.15 (-1.04 to 1.34) and on needle-passing it is -0.50
(-1.21 to -0.04) — that is, on needle-passing the fitted model is *worse* than predicting
the training mean, with an interval excluding zero on the wrong side. Under the
pre-declared rule, only knot-tying beats the reference. The zero-shot path behaves the same
way: +2.30 (1.14-3.45) on knot-tying, -0.05 (-1.05 to 0.96) on suturing and -0.24 (-0.79
to 0.31) on needle-passing. The two knot-tying reductions are not distinguishable from one
another: their intervals overlap across almost their whole length, and nothing in this
study ranks the two paths. Its fitted slope was positive in all 38 folds, so the direction
guard of §3.5 passed and no inverted score is being rescued by the calibration line.

Under LOSO the same comparison is positive on all three tasks for ridge (+1.15, +1.78,
+0.43, all intervals excluding zero), which is the §4.1 pattern reappearing in the
regression target.

Agreement statistics tell a consistent story. Under LOUO, ICC(2,1) is 0.60 (0.35-0.78) for
ridge and 0.73 (0.53-0.85) for the zero-shot path on knot-tying, and is indistinguishable
from zero on the other two tasks for both paths (ridge 0.20 and 0.00; zero-shot 0.06 and
-0.13). Bland-Altman bias is small everywhere (-0.37 to +0.52 GRS points), but the limits
of agreement span roughly ±11 points on suturing and needle-passing against ±7 on
knot-tying — that is, on two of three tasks the disagreement between model and rater is
about as large as the full spread of the scale. For context, pooled inter-rater reliability for
global rating scales in simulation-based assessment is ICC 0.78 (95% CI 0.71-0.83, 23
studies; Ilgen et al. 2015), with the original OSATS report giving 0.64-0.72 (Martin et
al. 1997). Our strongest agreement, 0.73 for the zero-shot path on knot-tying, sits below
that pooled figure and carries an interval (0.53-0.85) wide enough to be uninformative
about the comparison. We avoid the language of a ceiling: rater reliability is not a fixed
bar but depends heavily on training, with untrained raters reported at ICC 0.26, rising to
0.76 immediately after training but varying widely on later re-testing (-0.17 to 0.70),
which the authors summarise as the benefit of training being "lost in periods of nonuse"
(Gawad et al. 2019). The target these models
are fitted to is therefore itself noisy, and the reading we take is simply that no method
here demonstrated agreement comparable to trained human raters on any task.

We note that the pooled out-of-fold Spearman correlations for these same predictions
(LOUO: 0.21, 0.64, -0.13 for ridge) must be read against the feature-free reference's
values on the identical folds (-0.78, -0.86, -0.79) rather than against zero, for the
reason established in §3.7.

`kind=eval_agreement`, `kind=regression` *

### 4.4 A one-feature comparison [post-hoc]

This comparison was added after §4.3, to ask what the surviving knot-tying signal consists
of. It is diagnostic.

The zero-shot model's overall score correlates with total task time at Spearman -0.91
(suturing), -0.81 (knot-tying) and -0.86 (needle-passing). Correlations with all 16
features are logged, so this is not a selected maximum: task time is the strongest
associate in every task, with the next strongest between 0.41 and 0.71 in absolute value.

Substituting a single ordinary-least-squares line on task time alone for either model, on
identical folds and under the identical metric, reduces error by +1.88 GRS points
(0.94-2.89) on knot-tying under LOUO. On suturing (+0.10, -0.72 to 0.83) and
needle-passing (-0.29, -0.88 to 0.11) it fails to beat the reference, as both models do.
The three knot-tying intervals — 0.65-2.52 for ridge, 1.14-3.45 for the zero-shot path and
0.94-2.89 for task time alone — overlap across almost their whole length. We therefore
report them as three estimates of one effect and draw no ordering among them; the point
estimates differ, but this study cannot resolve that difference.

On this evidence the surviving cross-surgeon signal is, to within the resolution of eight
surgeons, the signal available from trial duration alone. We do not claim the two are
equivalent; we claim that this study does not demonstrate any advantage for either the
16-feature model or the zero-shot model over a one-feature baseline on the one task where a
signal survives.

### 4.5 Calibration [pre-declared]

Each path is assessed against the target it was given: the baseline classifier against the
self-declared skill label it was trained on, and the zero-shot model against the level
implied by GRS, raw and with no recalibration applied. The two are therefore not compared
with one another.

On a held-out surgeon, the baseline classifier states a mean confidence of 0.755 while
achieving 0.379 accuracy. Its expected calibration error is 0.377 (0.237-0.541), and its
Brier score of 0.875 is worse than the 0.647 obtained by a constant forecast equal to the
observed class frequencies — a Brier skill score of -0.351, with an interval (-0.688 to
-0.092) lying entirely below zero. Its most confident bin, containing 46 of 103 trials at
a mean stated confidence of 0.92, is correct 39% of the time (`addendum_reliability_bin`).

Under LOSO the same classifier appears well calibrated and, if anything, underconfident:
mean confidence 0.837 against accuracy 0.942, expected calibration error 0.105, Brier skill
+0.778 (0.698-0.856). The same model and the same features thus yield opposite calibration
verdicts under the two protocols, which is §4.1 restated in the confidence domain.

The zero-shot model is also overconfident: mean confidence 0.696 against accuracy 0.301,
expected calibration error 0.395 (0.317-0.494), Brier skill -0.433 (-0.588 to -0.242). Its
predictions are usually close — within one level on 84% of trials — but they sit
systematically high: the mean predicted level is 2.05 against a mean true level of 1.71, an
offset of about a third of a level, and it places 39 trials in the top two levels where the
GRS-implied truth places 22 (`addendum_jev_level_agreement`, `addendum_jev_level_usage`).
That offset means its exact-level miscalibration mixes genuine
overconfidence with a scale mismatch between its rubric and our GRS-derived levels; the two
cannot be separated without a recalibration that was excluded in advance, and we therefore
do not attribute the full -0.433 to overconfidence.

### 4.6 Selective prediction [pre-declared]

The deployment question is whether a model's confidence identifies the trials on which it
should defer to a human rater. We rank the 103 pooled trials by each path's own confidence,
defer the least confident first, and measure the absolute GRS error of those retained.

**The two paths do not support the same inference here, and we state that before the
numbers.** Ridge regression emits no native confidence, so some proxy was required; we
declared in advance that the baseline would pair its regression prediction with the
classifier's top-class probability. Those are two different models fitted to two different
targets, and §3.1 establishes that the targets disagree — one surgeon labelled expert
averages GRS 13.5 while one labelled novice averages 19.1. The baseline's confidence
therefore carries no information about the regression's error except whatever the two
targets happen to share. **Its null result is uninformative about whether ridge knows when
it is wrong; it is a property of the pairing.** The zero-shot path has no such problem:
its score and its confidence come from one model answering one question, so it is the only
clean test of self-knowledge in this study.

The clean test is null. Ordering by the zero-shot model's own confidence lowers the area
under the risk-coverage curve by 0.155 GRS points relative to random ordering under LOUO,
with a 95% interval of -0.416 to 0.606; substituting its maximum level probability for its
reported confidence changes nothing (0.149). Under LOSO the figure is 0.077. The
uninterpretable arm is also null, for the record: 0.135 (-0.628 to 0.922) under LOUO and
0.045 under LOSO. Expressed as a fraction of the distance between random and ideal
ordering these are 7.4% and 5.5% under LOUO, and 3.9% and 2.5% under LOSO — percentages of
a quantity that is itself indistinguishable from zero, so they should be read only
alongside the intervals above and not quoted alone.

The coverage table shows the same flatness directly. Deferring 20% of trials by baseline
confidence under LOUO changes the retained error from 4.31 to 4.50 GRS points — it rises —
and deferring 60% gives 4.15. The zero-shot path falls from 4.05 to 3.76 at 40% coverage,
a change no interval supports.

One further caution. The upper interval bounds are +0.61 GRS points for the clean arm and
+0.92 for the other, up to a fifth of the overall error, so this is an under-powered null
rather than a demonstration that confidence is useless. In summary, this study yields one
clean null and one uninterpretable one, not two independent confirmations. A predictor
with native uncertainty — quantile or Bayesian regression, or a conformal wrapper — would
remove the mismatch and is the concrete design a follow-up should adopt.

*Figure 3 (`eval_risk_coverage.png`); Table 5. Source: `kind=eval_selective`, `kind=eval_coverage`.*

### 4.7 Robustness

Conclusions do not depend on the filtering choice. Repeating the LOUO analysis on
unfiltered features and at an 8 Hz cutoff leaves knot-tying positive throughout (+1.18 to
+1.69 GRS points) and suturing and needle-passing non-significant throughout; knot-tying's
interval excludes zero in four of the six configuration-by-filter combinations, failing at
8 Hz (lower bound -0.26). Widening the ridge penalty grid in a second configuration leaves
knot-tying unchanged and moves suturing and needle-passing toward the reference, because
the inner search then selects maximal shrinkage; both configurations are reported.

The zero-shot model is close to, but not exactly, deterministic: three repetitions of an
identical request agree at Spearman 0.999 with a mean absolute score difference of
0.022-0.024 on a 0-4 scale, and only 19-21% of trials return a bit-identical score
(`addendum_jev_repeatability`). Averaging the three runs
changes every headline figure by at most 0.01 GRS points.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What the LOSO protocol measures

The three-way skill label in JIGSAWS is a property of the surgeon, not of the trial: it
records hours of robotic experience and is constant across every trial that surgeon
performs. Under LOSO, every surgeon in the test fold also appears in training. A model that
can recognise an individual can therefore retrieve that individual's label without
representing skill at all, and it will be scored as correct for doing so.

Our results are consistent with this happening. Changing only the protocol removes at least
a third of the reported accuracy and plausibly more than half (§4.1), and the same 16
features identify which of eight surgeons performed a held-out repetition with accuracy
0.83 to 0.93 against chance rates near 0.13 (§4.2). The information needed to shortcut the
task is demonstrably present in the feature representation, and it is present in quantity.

We are careful about what this establishes. We have shown that surgeon identity is
recoverable from these features and that accuracy collapses when identity stops being
useful. We have **not** shown that the classifier internally relies on identity: that would
require an intervention on the representation — for example, removing surgeon-discriminative
directions from the feature space and re-testing — which we did not perform. The
identifiability result is a sufficient mechanism and a post-hoc diagnostic, not a
confirmatory test of the classifier's internals. A reader is entitled to the weaker reading
that both the LOSO accuracy and the identifiability reflect some third property of
individuals that happens to correlate with the label, and our data do not separate those
accounts.

What does not depend on that distinction is the practical consequence. Whatever the
mechanism, a LOSO figure does not estimate performance on a surgeon the system has never
seen, which is the only situation in which an assessment tool would be deployed. The gap we
measure is the size of that mismatch on this dataset.

The structure is not specific to JIGSAWS. Any benchmark that predicts a person-level label
from repeated measurements of the same people, and that evaluates with those people present
in training, admits the same shortcut. Surgical skill assessment is an instance; so are many
clinical and behavioural benchmarks with few participants and several recordings each. We
do not claim to have measured the effect anywhere else, and a single dataset with eight
participants cannot establish how common it is.

### 5.2 What survives a held-out surgeon, and what it consists of

On a genuinely unseen surgeon, one task of three retains a signal. On knot-tying both paths
predict GRS better than a reference that simply predicts the training-fold mean, and on
suturing and needle-passing neither does; on needle-passing the fitted baseline is worse
than the reference (§4.3). The surviving effect is modest in absolute terms — roughly a
quarter to a third of the per-task standard deviation of GRS.

Two features of that surviving signal deserve emphasis. First, a single ordinary-least-
squares line on total task time reduces error on knot-tying by an amount whose interval
overlaps both models' across almost its whole length (§4.4). We therefore treat the three as
three estimates of one effect. This study does not demonstrate that 16 hand-crafted features
or a commercial decision model add anything over trial duration on the one task where
anything survives. Second, the zero-shot model's score correlates with task time at -0.81 to
-0.91, so the convergence is not a coincidence of fitting: the model's output largely *is* a
duration measure, expressed on an ordinal scale.

That result should be read with a disclosure. The rubric we wrote for the zero-shot model
describes completion time and pauses, and it was written after we had seen which features
carried signal, though before any trial was scored and without subsequent revision (§3.3).
This is a plausible route by which the model came to track duration, and we cannot exclude
it. What the comparison does support is narrower and still useful: a model given no training
examples, calibrated by two free parameters per fold, matches fitted alternatives on this
task — and so does a one-feature regression, which is the more economical explanation.

### 5.3 Confidence is not currently a basis for deferral

The deployment case for automated assessment usually assumes a human in the loop: the system
scores what it is confident about and refers the rest. That requires confidence to track
correctness. On a held-out surgeon it does not.

Both paths are overconfident. The baseline states a mean confidence of 0.755 and is correct
on 0.379 of trials; the zero-shot model states 0.696 and is correct on 0.301. Both score
worse than a constant forecast of the observed class frequencies, with Brier skill intervals
lying entirely below zero (§4.5). The failure is in the direction that matters: a system
that is confidently wrong on unfamiliar operators is worse for triage than one that is
uncertain everywhere, because the errors it passes through unreviewed are exactly the ones
it will not flag.

The same model looks well calibrated under LOSO — mean confidence 0.837 against accuracy
0.942, Brier skill +0.778. We read this as the §5.1 finding restated: apparent calibration,
like apparent accuracy, is manufactured by the protocol. A calibration audit conducted under
LOSO would have reported a well-behaved system.

On selective prediction the two arms are not equivalent evidence, and we state the asymmetry
plainly. Ridge has no native confidence, so the baseline pairs a regression prediction with a
classifier's probability — two models, two targets, and §3.1 shows those targets disagree.
**That arm's null result is uninformative about whether ridge knows when it is wrong.** The
zero-shot arm has no such defect, since its score and its confidence come from one model
answering one question, and it is also null: ordering by its own confidence recovers 0.155
GRS points with an interval of -0.416 to 0.606 (§4.6). This study therefore contributes one
clean null and one uninterpretable one, not two independent confirmations.

Both nulls are under-powered. The upper interval bounds reach +0.61 and +0.92 GRS points, up
to a fifth of the overall error, so a modest real benefit is entirely compatible with what we
observed. The correct summary is that we found no evidence that confidence identifies
deferrable trials, not that confidence is useless. Settling it needs either more surgeons or
a predictor with native uncertainty — quantile or Bayesian regression, or a conformal wrapper
— which would also remove the pairing defect. We regard that as the most concrete methodological
improvement a follow-up should adopt.

### 5.4 Implications for evaluation practice

Three changes would be cheap and would have caught what we found.

**Report the held-out-surgeon protocol as primary.** Both schemes ship with JIGSAWS and both
are commonly cited, but they answer different questions, and only one corresponds to
deployment. Where LOSO is reported, the gap to LOUO should be reported beside it, ideally as
a paired difference with an interval, since that is the quantity with a clear meaning.

**Anchor claims to an uninformative reference.** A correlation or an accuracy is not
self-interpreting. We found this twice: a feature-free predictor scores about -0.8 on pooled
rank correlation under LOUO purely by construction (§3.7), and a model can post a positive
correlation while having larger error than predicting the training mean. Reporting error
against an explicit reference predictor made both visible, and costs nothing.

**Publish a subject-identifiability diagnostic.** Training the same features to predict
participant identity is a few lines of code and directly estimates the size of the available
shortcut. We suggest it as routine for any benchmark with person-level labels and repeated
measurements, alongside a one-feature baseline — here, task time alone matched everything
else on the only task with a surviving signal.

We note the tension in making these recommendations from a study that itself substituted its
headline metric after seeing that the pre-registered one was uninterpretable, and that added
its central diagnostic post-hoc (§3.8, §4). We have labelled both rather than presenting the
final analysis as the planned one. The recommendations stand on the mechanism, not on our
adherence.

### 5.5 Scope

This is a single-dataset case study. Eight surgeons — seven on one task — make every
interval crude, and all intervals here express which surgeons were evaluated, not how results
would move under refitting, and not the sampling of those eight individuals from any wider
population. We examined one conventional baseline and one commercial decision model at one
pinned version, with one rubric wording, on one benchmark. We did not reproduce any published
system, so our claims concern the protocol's structure and our own demonstration of it, not
the validity of specific prior results.

The confound and the calibration failure are demonstrated here, not established as general.
Both should be checked on a second dataset with more participants before being treated as
properties of the field rather than of this benchmark. What we would regard as settled is
narrower: on JIGSAWS, with these features, the choice of evaluation protocol changes reported
skill-classification accuracy by at least a third, the same features identify the operator
far above chance, and neither model's stated confidence was shown to identify the trials it
gets wrong.
